# Hardware characterization — final research summary

The Intel Xeon CPU Max 9480 on CRESCO8 delivered about **2.1× as much local read bandwidth on HBM as on DDR** on one Flat-mode socket. The gain for the measured decode-like projections was **1.43× in plain BF16 and 1.51× in prepacked INT8**. At M = 4096 it narrowed to about 7% and less than 1%, respectively. NUMA placement, working-set behavior and the achieved library rate matter more to these results than the advertised HBM maximum or the tile peak alone. [Master analysis](../notebooks/00_master_analysis.ipynb) · [Evidence matrix](SCIENTIFIC_CLAIMS.md)

## Architecture and measurement

The measured hosts have two 56-core Sapphire Rapids sockets with SMT disabled. Each socket has 48 KiB L1D and 2 MiB L2 per core, 112.5 MiB shared L3, 64 GiB HBM2e and 512 GiB installed DDR5 across eight channels per socket. Flat mode exposes a local and a remote target for each memory tier. Cache mode uses HBM as a transparent, direct-mapped, write-back cache in front of DDR. HBM-only and SNC4 appear as external context. The SKU's DDR5-4800 one-DIMM-per-channel interface calculation is 307.2 GB/s per socket. HMAT's 869 GB/s HBM read entry describes firmware metadata. [Platform records](01_SCOPE_AND_TOPOLOGY.md) · [Intel tuning guide, §§2–3](https://cdrdv2-public.intel.com/782255/354227-intel-xeon-cpu-max-series-configuration-and-tuning-guide-1.pdf) · [Intel SKU specifications](https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/products/sku/232592/intel-xeon-cpu-max-9480-processor-112-5m-cache-1-90-ghz/specifications.html)

Working sets are in binary GiB and bandwidth is in decimal GB/s. Use the window median and its observed spread for each launch. Between-launch comparisons are a separate check and several campaigns share a host or allocation. Useful stream rates follow bytes implied by the benchmark. Flat controller rates estimate CAS traffic from complete in-window intervals, including background. Cache uses upstream lookup bytes for effective bandwidth and tracks DDR fills separately. The tag-event validation was intrusive, so production Cache measurements use the base counters. [Methods](03_METHODS_AND_DATA.md) · [Counters](06_COUNTERS.md)

## Why sustained HBM bandwidth is lower than the advertised figure

Intel's series overview says “Up to 1TB/s BW” in the HBM entry adjacent to the per-socket capacity. This is a rounded product-level reference of 1,000 GB/s per socket. Intel gives no benchmark procedure in that entry. McCalpin's separate interface calculation for the tested Xeon Max stacks is 1,638.4 GB/s per socket at a stated 3.2 GT/s over four 128-byte-wide interfaces. The installed CRESCO8 stack transfer rate was not measured. A CPU stream traverses the cores, caches, mesh and controllers, and its read/write mix, timing and degree of concurrency affect useful bandwidth. [Intel series overview](https://www.intel.com/content/www/us/en/developer/articles/technical/xeon-scalable-processor-max-series.html) · [McCalpin, slides 3–5](https://www.ixpug.org/images/docs/ISC23/McCalpin_SPR_BW_limits_2023-05-24_final.pdf)

- **Steady bandwidth.** The 8 GiB useful read reaches about 543 GB/s HBM and 262 GB/s DDR on socket 0. The counted Flat Roofline references are 553 and 261 GB/s from a separate controller-counted run. In E6, changing the read data from random or varied to constants or zeros raises controller bandwidth from about 531–532 to 579–583 GB/s. The unmodified STREAM implementation reaches 678–711 GB/s with selected builds using its best-iteration convention. That reproduces the vicinity of McCalpin's 700 GB/s Triad on this host. Loop unrolling, store compilation, array size, initial values and the choice of statistic account for different parts of the measured benchmark gap. The effects need not add across conditions. [STREAMGAP E1–E6](STREAMGAP.md)
- **Power and clocks.** U1 records a drop from 2.57 to 1.68 GHz core and 2.50 to 1.54 GHz uncore between 14 and 56 HBM-reading cores. At 56 cores the package draws about 350 W and the PCU reports power clipping throughout, with no detected thermal clipping. The first counted HBM read window draws 377 W and serves about 13% more controller traffic, with an 11% faster uncore and almost unchanged core clock. DDR has a power transient with practically unchanged bandwidth. E6 changes only initialization pattern and observes a proportional change in HBM bandwidth and uncore clock under power limiting. E7c changes core count and traffic mix. Below the full socket it records slower throughput despite faster uncore, consistent with insufficient request concurrency. E7c also records mismatches between modeled bytes and CAS traffic, particularly with ordinary stores. [Notebook 06](../notebooks/06_uncore_power_and_clocks.ipynb) · [STREAMGAP E7–E7c](STREAMGAP.md)
- **Defensible mechanism.** Active package power limiting constrains the observed operating point. HBM throughput is sensitive to uncore frequency in the measured high-load controls. Request concurrency and loaded latency provide a complementary limit. Using the measured 209 ns chase as a latency proxy, Little's law implies about 33 outstanding lines per streaming core at 549 GB/s. A socket would need about 58 per core to reach 1,000 GB/s at the same latency with 56 cores. This is a conditional estimate, and McCalpin measured about 32 at full load on his system. The available interventions do not isolate the effect of raising PL1 or fixing uncore frequency. They leave the relative roles of mesh links, controller queues and component-level power unresolved. The newer exclusive Slurm search reports about 362–363 W on its busiest packages and lacks a PL1 readback. Its higher rates should be reported as a separate operating point. [McCalpin, slides 14–18](https://www.ixpug.org/images/docs/ISC23/McCalpin_SPR_BW_limits_2023-05-24_final.pdf) · [Completed Flat search](BANDWIDTH_CONFIGURATION_STUDY.md)

Reguly reported 1,446 GB/s, or 1,643 GB/s with streaming stores, for two-socket BabelStream Triad in HBM-only mode with SNC4 and SMT enabled. The comparison helps establish configuration sensitivity. It mixes benchmark, memory mode, clustering and thread policy. McCalpin's 555 GB/s one-socket read figure is closer to the selected local Flat read scope. [Reguly, §2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.09084) · [Literature inputs](../configs/literature.toml)

## Latency, capacity and placement

HBM idle dependent-load latency was 139 ns at 1 GiB against DDR's 116 ns in the paired test. With 55 streaming cores it was 209 ns at 549 GB/s, against DDR's 373 ns at 262 GB/s. DDR read throughput was already near saturation at 28 cores. HBM gains substantially from 28 to 56 cores. These tests support HBM for sufficiently concurrent local work and distinguish throughput from dependent-load latency. [Memory analysis](../notebooks/02_memory_hierarchy_and_bandwidth.ipynb)

At 8, 64 and 128 GiB, the counted Cache read launch delivers about 546, 134 and 82 GB/s in lookup bytes with 92%, 45% and 12% hits. DDR fill traffic is visible well below 64 GiB. At 56 GiB the earlier 531 GB/s launch and four later launches near 149–153 GB/s demonstrate state sensitivity. Their difference is consistent with physical-address conflicts in a direct-mapped cache. Hit counters are unavailable for the earlier launch. At 96 GiB several GEMMs exceed the read-only lookup reference, consistent with different DDR fill rates at similar hit fractions. [Cache counters and repeats](../notebooks/06_uncore_power_and_clocks.ipynb)

The repeated 56-core remote HBM read reaches about 46.5 GB/s versus approximately 539 GB/s local on socket 0. A counted repeat sees excess HBM reads and UPI data flits per useful line. Aggregate counters leave the issuing agent unknown. Independent socket-local workers attain 0.984–1.017× the sum of isolated rates across 29 paired cases. A 112-thread reader gives about 1.10 TB/s with first-touch locality and 265 GB/s with interleaving in the October run. The November run gives 1.16 TB/s and 254 GB/s, respectively. These results describe concurrent local work and the placement cost of the tested remote traffic. [NUMA analysis](../notebooks/03_numa_and_socket_scaling.ipynb) · [November search](../notebooks/07_bandwidth_configuration.ipynb)

## Native compute and projection regimes

At 56 cores the native Flat register kernels sustain 4.41 TFLOP/s FP64, 8.82 TFLOP/s FP32, 4.18 TFLOP/s AVX-512 BF16, 35.91 T integer op/s VNNI, 112.95 TFLOP/s AMX BF16 and 225.92 T integer op/s AMX INT8. They achieve 94–99% of the relevant per-cycle references at effective clocks of 1.99–2.59 GHz. The register-only AMX measurements ran below the observed PL1 threshold. A large GEMM has different power, instruction and memory demands. [Compute analysis](../notebooks/04_compute_throughput.ipynb) · [ISA reference](../configs/theory.toml)

The empirical Roofline uses useful matrix work `2MNK`, intensity in operations per counted byte, `P(I) = min(P_tile, B × I)` and ridge `P_tile / B`. Flat HBM/DDR ridges are 204/432 op/B for AMX BF16 and 409/864 op/B for AMX INT8. M = 1 is at about 1.01 FLOP/B in plain BF16 and 2.04 integer op/B in prepacked INT8. HBM improves the two projection rates from 0.229 to 0.327 TFLOP/s and from 0.471 to 0.713 T integer op/s, respectively. Larger batches increase reuse. The BF16 HBM intensity at M = 4096 is about 486 FLOP/B against 1,792 modeled, and its measured M = 256–4096 range is 181–507 FLOP/B. Aggregate traffic exceeds the one-pass tensor model, with rereads, packing and scratch traffic possible contributors. HBM reaches 39.26 TFLOP/s BF16 and 70.48 T integer op/s INT8 at M = 4096, around 35% and 31% of the corresponding native peaks. Plain BF16 crosses its reference ridge at M = 512 on HBM and M = 1024 on DDR. INT8 crosses at M = 256 on HBM and remains below its DDR ridge through M = 4096. The crossing changes the smaller term in the model. The library still sustains a fraction of the tile rate. [Projection results](../data/processed/thesis/principal_gemm.csv) · [Roofline atlas](../notebooks/05_roofline_atlas.ipynb) · [Williams et al.](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/5tz795vq)

At full local read rate, gross RAPL package plus DDR energy per controller byte is approximately 679 versus 1,625 pJ/B at the computing socket and 1,008 versus 2,398 pJ/B for both sockets, HBM versus DDR. The second socket draws power even when it runs no benchmark threads. The idle reference is roughly 310–315 W at node RAPL scope and 400 W at the BMC wall scope. These results support a workload- and scope-specific energy comparison. The benchmark is a projection study with synthetic weights. Model tokens/s and joules/token computed by applying one projection's rate to the full parameter count describe conditional scenarios, and the chapter's principal figures use measured projection rates.
